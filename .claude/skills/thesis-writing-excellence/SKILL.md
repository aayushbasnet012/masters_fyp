# Thesis Writing Excellence

## What this is

A rubric distilled from annotating a real distinction-grade MSc thesis line by line. The annotations named the exact moves that separated it from merely competent writing: precise gap statements, dual (academic + practical) contribution framing, ethics threaded through every section rather than boxed into one chapter, honest limitations, and so on. This file turns those annotations into a checklist you can apply to any thesis.

Treat the source thesis's topic as illustrative only. The techniques below are domain-independent; apply them to whatever the user is actually writing about.

## Three modes

1. **Critique mode** — the user pastes existing thesis text and wants feedback. Read it against the relevant section checklist below, name what's missing or weak with a specific fix, and don't pad the response with praise for things that are merely adequate. Match the user's stated tone preference (if conversational and concise elsewhere, don't switch to a listy report for a two-paragraph excerpt).
2. **Draft mode** — the user wants a section written or rewritten (research aim, objectives, RQs, a case study, etc.). Write it directly, applying the section's checklist as you go, rather than writing generic prose and hoping it fits.
3. **Structure-check mode** — the user wants to know if their overall thesis skeleton is sound (right sections, right order, nothing missing). Compare against the Standard Skeleton below.

Most requests are critique or draft mode on a single section — don't force a full structure-check when the user only shared one paragraph.

## The five signal categories

Every strong sentence in the source thesis did one of five jobs. When drafting, aim to hit several of these per section; when critiquing, name which ones are present and which are missing.

- **Academic voice / original claims** — active, ownership-claiming statements of contribution ("This thesis proposes...", "This project seeks to bridge that gap"), paired with passive voice for observable facts ("State actual facts"). The mix matters: passive for what's true regardless of the author, active for what the author is doing.
- **Specificity & evidence** — exact numbers, named studies, named companies, dates. "55–60% accuracy" and "positive ROI at ≥70% confidence" beat "the model performed reasonably well." Vague quantifiers ("many", "several", "significantly") are a tell that a claim hasn't been checked.
- **Domain knowledge** — vocabulary that only someone who has actually worked in the field would use correctly ("algorithmic opacity" in AI ethics, "loss aversion" in behavioral economics). Generic ML/business-speak in a specialist section is a red flag that the writer is skimming the surface.
- **Gap identification & critical analysis** — every major claim of originality needs one precise sentence stating what's missing from prior work, and it must be precise: not "not much research exists on X" but "hasn't been applied in an open, transparent, academic manner." A gap stated twice (once early, once in the justification) reinforces it — but only if it's the same precise gap both times, not a vaguer restatement.
- **Interdisciplinary / theoretical connections** — theory used to justify design decisions, not decoration. "A data-driven model, by remaining emotionless, is uniquely positioned to detect these inefficiencies" turns Prospect Theory into a reason for the technical approach. If a cited theory doesn't change what the researcher built or measured, it's not doing its job yet.

## Recurring structural motifs

Beyond the section-by-section content, the source thesis reuses a handful of structural devices across multiple chapters. These are easy to miss because they're not tied to any single section — apply them wherever the shoe fits.

- **Two-pass theory.** When a theory matters both as motivation and as scholarly grounding, use it twice, deliberately: once early and informally, uncited, to justify why the problem matters; again later in the literature review, now cited, with named sub-concepts and definitions. Don't cut the early pass just because the theory reappears later — the two passes do different jobs (motivate vs. ground) and a reader who only sees the cited version misses the motivational payoff.
- **Roadmap-then-detail.** Before a long multi-part section (most often the literature review), open with a short explicit list naming the parts to come, in order, before writing any of them. One sentence or a short list — not a paragraph. It's a cheap, visible signal that the field or topic was mapped comprehensively before writing began.
- **Shared template across parallel items.** When a thesis includes multiple instances of the same kind of thing — case studies, sprints, experiments — give every instance the identical internal structure (e.g., Introduction → Issues → Reasons for success/limitations → Conclusion for each case study). Parallel items with inconsistent internal shapes read as separately bolted-on essays rather than a deliberate comparative set.
- **Explicit pairing labels.** Where two things are meant to map onto each other one-to-one (a hypothesis and its research question, a finding and its research question), say so in the label itself — "Hypothesis 1 (for Research Question 1)" — rather than leaving the reader to infer the pairing from order alone.
- **Restate before you answer.** Any subsection whose whole job is to answer a previously-posed question (most often a findings subsection) should restate or closely paraphrase that question first, then answer it in the very next sentence. Don't assume the reader remembers the exact wording from three chapters earlier.
- **Design-rationale asides.** Occasionally name the reasoning behind a structural choice directly in the text — e.g., a short explicit note on why there are exactly two research questions rather than five. Use this sparingly, once or twice per thesis, at a genuinely load-bearing decision point. Overuse turns confident design into anxious self-justification.
- **Visual overview for technical/systems work.** If the thesis has a pipeline, architecture, or a cyclical methodology worth seeing at a glance, add one or two diagrams positioned before the written sections they support (e.g., an end-to-end system architecture, a methodology/sprint cycle). Optional — most valuable when the work has a clear technical pipeline or an iterative process that's easier to grasp visually than in prose.

## Section-by-section checklist

Use this as a lookup, not a script — not every thesis will have every section, and the labels here (e.g. "problem context", "justification") map to whatever the user's own chapter names are.

**Introduction** — funnel structure (broad context → specific problem → the thesis's proposed solution, in that order), threading in domain-specific vocabulary as it narrows (not generic language) to signal substantive understanding from the first paragraph. Use passive voice for observable facts and active voice for the contribution claim, both within the same paragraph. One explicit gap-statement sentence. Close with a directly-owned claim of contribution ("This thesis proposes...").

**Problem context / motivation** — sharpen the gap from the introduction with one more layer of specificity, stated as its own explicit gap sentence. If the field is interdisciplinary, name the 2-3 disciplines explicitly in one sentence rather than gesturing at "various fields." If a theory (e.g. behavioural economics) will be formally introduced later in the literature review, consider referencing its core biases informally here first — this doubles as the informal first pass of "Two-pass theory" (see Recurring structural motifs).

**Theoretical framing** (economic theory, prior technical work, adjacent-field validation, etc.) — theories/prior art must be applied, not just cited. Cite landmark/primary sources precisely and accurately (e.g. "Kahneman and Tversky, 1979") — accurate citation of a primary source signals real engagement with the literature, not a textbook summary. Name specific concepts with brief definitions (aim for 2-3, not 1 and not 6 — enough to show depth, not so many it becomes a list dump). End with a conceptual bridge sentence connecting the theory directly to a design decision in this thesis. If the same theory will resurface in the literature review, treat this first pass as the informal/motivational one — see "Two-pass theory" above.

**Adjacent-field or prior-art validation** (e.g. "Role of Data and Machine Learning") — cross-domain validation before the domain-specific claim: show the approach already works in 2-3 unrelated fields (finance, healthcare, logistics, etc.) to preempt the "does this even work" objection before applying it to this thesis's own domain. Use advanced, cutting-edge domain vocabulary (not just basic/generic terms) to signal the researcher operates at the current edge of the field. State the gap with precision — the gap is rarely "no one has used X," it's usually "X hasn't been applied in [specific manner]" (e.g. "in an open, transparent, and academic manner"). Close with a short, confident, active claim of intent to bridge that gap ("This project seeks to bridge that gap") — unqualified and direct.

**Research aim** — exactly one sentence. Broader than any single objective, narrower than the general topic area. If it takes more than one sentence, it's actually objectives in disguise.

**Objectives** — a numbered list decomposing the aim. A reliable shape: (1) domain foundation — understand the domain/factors at play, so later technical decisions don't appear arbitrary, (2) literature ground — survey what techniques exist and how they've been used elsewhere, feeding directly into which specific methods get chosen later (don't let this be decorative — the algorithm choices in the tools section should trace back to this objective's findings), (3) gap identification — identify the specific gap that justifies the thesis's claim to an original contribution, (4) the core build — design, develop, and evaluate the actual artefact, mapping directly to the primary/technical research question, (5) responsible reporting — document honestly (limitations, licensing e.g. Creative Commons, reproducibility, dissemination). Objectives describe *actions*; if one reads like a prediction of an outcome, it belongs in the hypotheses instead.

**Contribution & significance** — state the contribution twice, explicitly labeled: once as an academic contribution (what gap in the literature this fills) and once as a practical one (what a practitioner could do with this) — keep these as two separate sentences, don't merge them; the separation is itself evidence the thesis understands it must produce both scholarly knowledge and applicable value. Where relevant, add a social/equity angle (e.g. how the work democratizes access to something previously gatekept) as a third, distinct claim connecting the technical work to broader relevance beyond the domain and the practitioner. Close with one sentence of broader applicability beyond the immediate domain.

**Justification of the study** — build the case from multiple independent angles (aim for 4-6: technological readiness/timing, academic, interdisciplinary, practical, ethical are the recurring ones). Each angle should be a genuinely different argument, not a rephrasing — naming more disciplines here than were named in the problem context section is a legitimate progression, not an inconsistency, as the interdisciplinary case deepens through the thesis. Consider reiterating the research gap here with a specific evidentiary claim (not just repeating the earlier sentence verbatim) to reinforce its centrality. A sophisticated, original framing borrowed from an adjacent field (e.g. applying portfolio theory's risk-adjusted-returns logic to justify a "small, consistent gains" approach) is a strong signal of synthesis, not decoration. Include at least one realistic/non-overclaiming qualifier ("not a guarantee of X") — its absence is the single most common way student justifications read as naive.

**Research questions** — as few as fully cover the work, not as many as can be brainstormed. Two is often right for a technical + ethical/social split — check whether the programme/institution has an explicit expectation here (e.g. a responsible-AI or ethics requirement); if so, frame the ethical RQ as the genuine scholarly inquiry it enables, not a compliance checkbox. Every RQ must be traceable: it needs one or more matching objectives, a methodology step that answers it, and a findings subsection that answers it directly. Checked together, the RQ set should leave no major section of the thesis without a question it's answering — that's what makes a minimal RQ count read as complete rather than thin. An RQ that isn't answered anywhere in the findings shouldn't be there. If the RQ count is a deliberate, non-obvious choice, consider a short design-rationale aside explaining why (see above) — don't overuse this device elsewhere.

**Hypotheses** — distinct from objectives: objectives describe what the researcher will do, hypotheses predict what the researcher expects to find, and findings later confirm or complicate them with specific numbers. Label each hypothesis against its RQ explicitly ("Hypothesis 1 (for Research Question 1)") rather than leaving the pairing implicit. A conditional structure ("while X may improve Y, without Z, harm W may follow") is more sophisticated than a flat prediction for anything with a risk/ethics dimension. Keeping hypotheses and objectives clearly separate — rather than blurring them into one list — is itself a signal of research-design literacy at distinction level; don't collapse the distinction for the sake of brevity.

**Methodology** — name it (don't leave it as unlabeled "the approach used"); a named methodology signals methodological literacy and intentional design, not something stumbled into. Show triangulation — multiple independent sources or methods that can cross-check each other. Evaluate on more than one metric (e.g., not just accuracy, but also cost/profitability/interpretability/fairness — whatever's domain-appropriate); this is often the thesis's actual methodological innovation if prior work in the field stopped at one metric. If the methodology is iterative (sprints, cycles, phases), embed ethics as a check within every iteration rather than reporting it after the fact — align this explicitly with a named external framework (e.g. IEEE, EU AI ethics guidelines) where one exists, rather than inventing an ad hoc standard. State the method's own limitations honestly in the same section, not deferred to a conclusion — examiners read this as maturity, not weakness. If the methodology involves a pipeline or a repeating cycle, consider whether a diagram (see "Visual overview" above) would help before the reader hits the prose description.

**Ethics** — should not live in one chapter alone. Look for it recurring in the methodology (as a check built into each stage), in the technical/tools section (choices justified partly on ethical grounds, e.g. picking an interpretable model over a marginally more accurate black box), and in the findings (revisited, not just introduced). Vague promises ("we will act responsibly") are weak; name concrete mechanisms (a specific license, a specific confidence threshold, a specific disclaimer). Argue each concern on its own ethical reasoning rather than leaning on a compliance buzzword as a shortcut (e.g. make the privacy argument stand without the word "GDPR"; save the named regulation for the technical section where actual compliance mechanisms belong). If one sentence captures the thesis's core ethical stance, repeat it verbatim in more than one chapter — literal repetition of a load-bearing phrase, not just the same theme restated, is what creates argumentative coherence.

**Literature review** — open with a short roadmap naming the strands to come (2-4, in order) before writing any of them. If one strand (e.g. ethics) is meant to run through the whole review rather than living only in its own section, plant it as a theme in the very first paragraph, not just in its dedicated strand. If the roadmap promises a specific structure for recurring items later (e.g. "each case study will cover implementation, ethical dimensions, and outcomes"), that promise must actually be honored when those items appear — see "Shared template across parallel items" in Recurring structural motifs. Organize by theme/strand, not as a chronological list of paper summaries (though a chronological build *within* a strand, showing how the field evolved, is good). Every cited work needs a critical evaluation against a stated criterion ("lacks the complexity to account for non-linear relationships") — summarizing what a paper did without saying what it's missing is not literature review, it's an annotated bibliography. A theory-focused strand (e.g. behavioural economics) needs the same standard as the Theoretical framing section: apply each theory to a specific pattern or finding, don't just define it, and close the strand with a bridge sentence turning the theory into a research-design rationale — this doubles as that strand's gap sentence. Close each strand, and the review as a whole, with an explicit gap sentence.

**Case studies / real-world examples** — two to three is the usual range: enough to show a pattern, not so many the section drags. Give every case study the identical internal shape (e.g., Introduction → Issues → Reasons for success/limitations → Conclusion) so they read as a deliberate comparative set, not differently-organized essays stapled together. Give equal weight to success and critique; a case study that only praises reads as fan commentary, not scholarship. Use specific, checkable evidence (names, dates, numbers) — vague case studies are worse than no case study. Every case must feed back into the thesis's own design choices explicitly ("this directly parallels the agile methodology adopted here") — a case study that doesn't inform methodology is decorative and should be cut or connected. Consider including at least one case with a genuinely mixed or partial-failure outcome — three uniform success stories are less credible than two successes and one cautionary tale.

**Synthesis paragraphs** (end of lit review, end of a multi-part section) — must connect the threads into one argument, never restate them as a list. A good synthesis holds two things in tension at once (e.g., "these cases are both a foundation and a caution") rather than picking one conclusion. Where possible, end by connecting directly back to the thesis's own contribution/aim framing (e.g. echoing "effective and responsible" language used elsewhere) — this hands the baton from the literature to the thesis's own claim rather than leaving the synthesis as a dead end.

**Tools / technical implementation** — justify every tool choice with a reason, not just a list of what was used. Call out at least one choice explicitly as partly an ethical decision (e.g., interpretable model over marginally-better black box) — state the ethical principle as a technical design constraint ("transparency was prioritized over marginal accuracy gains"), not a separate moral aside; this is usually the single strongest ethics-in-practice sentence in a technical thesis. Name real challenges encountered with specific technical detail, not "several challenges arose."

**Ethical reflection in development** — distinct from the earlier Ethics section: this is where ethics gets revisited after the build, checked against what was actually implemented rather than what was planned. Frame it as a continuous parallel process running alongside every development phase, not a post-hoc review bolted on at the end. Apply precise ethics-literature concepts correctly to the technical domain (e.g. epistemic responsibility — users understanding not just what a model predicts but why). Reframe compliance requirements (GDPR, data protection) as deliberate design choices made, not constraints suffered. Acknowledge any limitation that survives mitigation as a persistent limitation, honestly, rather than implying it was fully resolved — this is read as scientific integrity, not a weakness. Close with a concrete, checkable safeguard (a named license with specific terms, e.g. Creative Commons with commercial-reuse restrictions) rather than a vague assurance about "responsible use."

**Findings** — restate or closely paraphrase the research question first, then directly answer it in the very next sentence — don't bury the answer and don't assume the reader remembers the RQ's exact wording. Report exact, honest numbers; suspiciously round or generous numbers undermine credibility. State the single most original insight as one quotable sentence — this is usually the sentence an examiner will remember. Name specific limitations plainly (not "some limitations exist" — say which ones and why). If the methodology made a specific claim about its own process (e.g. an agile method's promise of iterative, adaptive learning), consider whether the findings can validate that claim directly — showing the methodology produced what it promised is a distinct, worthwhile point separate from the technical results themselves.

**Findings for an ethical/social RQ specifically** — reuse the precise psychological or ethical vocabulary already established in the literature review (e.g. specific cognitive biases, named ethical concepts) as analytical tools here, rather than introducing new terms late; this is what makes the ethical findings read as an application of earlier work, not a bolt-on. Don't stop at diagnosing harm — propose constructive pathways forward (specific mechanisms, not "more research is needed"); critique alone is less sophisticated than critique-plus-mitigation. Ground the argument in named, internationally recognised bodies or frameworks (e.g. IEEE, UNESCO, relevant regulatory bodies) rather than personal opinion. If the section makes a normative claim (a statement about what *ought* to be, not just what *is*), state it explicitly and back it with the evidence assembled in the section — implicit or hedged normative claims read as less mature than a stated, evidenced one.

**Conclusion & future work** — recap the contribution in the same dual (academic + practical) framing used earlier, don't introduce a new framing at the end; consider echoing the introduction's or aim's exact language (not just its theme) to create a structural loop from open to close. Revisit ethics explicitly, using the same load-bearing phrase used elsewhere if one exists — don't drop it or soften it into a generic closing remark. Future work should be specific to what this study revealed as a gap, not a generic "more research is needed" — grouping distinct future-work directions into a small taxonomy (e.g. technical, practical, social, structural) shows more systematic thinking than a flat list. Consider closing with one broad, principled statement that transcends the immediate domain — this is often the sentence an examiner remembers most, separate from the technical recap.

## Anti-patterns (things that read as weak, or as AI-generated, regardless of section)

- No limitations anywhere, or limitations only in the conclusion instead of where they're relevant.
- A gap statement that's vague ("this hasn't been studied much") instead of precise ("hasn't been applied in an open, transparent, academic manner").
- Case studies or literature summaries with no critical evaluation — pure description.
- Case studies (or other parallel items) each organized with a different internal structure instead of a shared, deliberate template.
- Bullet lists doing work that synthesis prose should do — if a paragraph could be three bullet points instead, it hasn't actually connected the ideas yet.
- Objectives and hypotheses that say the same thing in different words.
- Rigid parallel constructions repeated back to back ("The first predicts X... The second predicts Y...", "not only A but also B" stacked sentence after sentence) — a strong human tell is *uneven* sentence rhythm, not perfect symmetry.
- Throat-clearing before a claim ("A fundamental question emerges that...") instead of just stating the claim.
- Specific, checkable factual claims (statistics, percentages, named reports) with no citation, or a citation that doesn't match the number when checked.

## How to give feedback (critique mode)

Be direct and specific: quote or point to the exact sentence, name which checklist item is missing or satisfied, and give the fix in-line rather than describing the fix abstractly. Don't manufacture equal amounts of praise and criticism — if a section is mostly solid, say so briefly and spend the space on what actually needs to change. If a factual or statistical claim looks checkable (a percentage, a named report, a market size), verify it before commenting rather than assuming it's right or wrong.

## Standard skeleton (structure-check mode)

The source thesis (and Coventry-affiliated MSc programmes generally) followed this shape. Other institutions vary the labels, but the underlying logic — motivate, justify, ask, predict, do, reflect — is transferable:

1. Introduction (funnel: context → gap → proposed solution)
2. Problem context & motivation
3. Theoretical/disciplinary framing (informal first pass — see "Two-pass theory")
4. Adjacent-field or prior-art validation
5. Research aim (one sentence)
6. Research objectives (numbered)
7. Contribution & significance (academic + practical)
8. Justification of the study (multi-angle)
9. Research questions (as few as needed, each traceable)
10. Hypotheses (distinct from objectives, explicitly labeled against their RQ)
11. Methodology (named, triangulated, multi-metric, limitations stated)
12. Ethical considerations
13. Literature review (roadmap → strand structure with the theory's cited second pass → case studies, shared template → synthesis)
14. Tools & technologies / implementation
15. Ethical reflection in development
16. Findings — one subsection per research question, each restating the RQ before answering it
17. Conclusion & future work

Optional: one or two architecture/process diagrams positioned before section 2, if the work has a pipeline or cyclical methodology worth seeing at a glance (see "Visual overview" above).
